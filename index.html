I study the political economy of development, particularly how states and other governing actors translate territorial authority into effective control over economic activity.

My research focuses primarily on Latin America and combines causal inference, spatial analysis, and original data collection.

Fiscal Extraction under Partial Sovereignty: Evidence from Criminal Territories in Rio de Janeiro

Abstract

What determines when territorial control translates into the power to tax? This paper studies taxation when assumptions of sovereignty are relaxed. I argue that under partial sovereignty, when border control is imperfect and states cannot rely on cooperative institutions, taxation requires demand captivity: rulers can only tax economic activity within their grasp.

I test this argument in criminal territories in Rio de Janeiro using two comparable fuels: liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and gasoline. LPG is consumed at home and transported in heavy cylinders, often on foot, whereas gasoline is mobile and can be purchased at any fuel station across the city. Using staggered territorial takeovers, I show that takeovers increase LPG retail prices and gross margins, while producing no comparable effect on gasoline prices and margins, despite both goods being sold inside criminal territory. These findings show that under partial sovereignty, territorial control enables extraction only when economic activity is spatially captive.

Research

Fiscal Extraction under Partial Sovereignty: Evidence from Criminal Territories in Rio de Janeiro

Abstract

What determines when territorial control translates into fiscal extraction? This paper studies fiscal capacity when assumptions of sovereignty are relaxed. I argue that under partial sovereignty, when border control is imperfect and local rulers cannot rely on cooperative institutions to enforce fiscal claims outside of their territory, fiscal extraction requires demand captivity: control over territory only translates into extractive power when consumption is spatially fixed and cannot be moved outside the territory. I test this argument in criminal territories in Rio de Janeiro using two comparable goods regulated by the same agency that differ in their place of consumption. Liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) is consumed at home and transported in heavy, visible cylinders, whereas gasoline can be purchased and pumped at any gas station across the city. Combining retail and wholesale price data with historical armed group maps, I leverage staggered first-observed transitions of retailer locations into criminal governance to estimate the effect on prices and margins. Among vendors that report acquisition costs, these transitions increase estimated gross margins by 20 percent, and overall LPG retail prices by 2 percent, while producing no comparable effect on gasoline. These findings extend the fiscal capacity literature, which has focused on the taxation of production, to the taxation of consumption and corroborate that captivity is the limiting factor on revenue extraction under conditions of partial sovereignty.
